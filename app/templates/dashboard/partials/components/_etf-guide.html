Understanding {{ etf_kind }} ETFs

What is an ETF?

An exchange-traded fund (ETF) is a fund that holds a basket of assets, such as stocks{{ ', company shares or crypto' if etf_kind == 'Crypto' }}, and trades on a stock exchange like a single share. Buying one share gives you a slice of everything the fund holds.

  • Diversified in one purchase. One fund can spread your money across dozens or hundreds of holdings.
  • Priced through the day. ETF prices move while the US market is open (9:30am–4:00pm New York time, weekdays).
  • Low running costs. Funds charge a small yearly fee (the "expense ratio"), taken from the fund's assets rather than billed to you.
{% if etf_kind == 'Crypto' %}

Three kinds of crypto ETF

  • Spot funds (IBIT, ETHA, FBTC) hold the coin itself, so they track its price closely.
  • Futures funds (BITO) hold bitcoin futures contracts. Rolling those contracts each month has a cost, so over time the fund can lag bitcoin.
  • Company funds (BITQ, BLOK) hold shares of crypto and blockchain businesses. They tend to move with crypto, but also with the stock market and each company's own results.

How buying works on StocksCo

  1. Choose a fund in Quick Invest and enter how much you want to invest.
  2. We buy whole shares at the latest price. Any amount left over stays in your wallet.
  3. The cost is paid from your wallet balance, so deposit first if you need to.
  4. Your shares appear in Portfolio, valued at the latest price. Prices refresh every few minutes.

Risks to know

  • An ETF's value can fall as well as rise, and you can get back less than you put in.
  • Diversification spreads risk across holdings, but it doesn't protect you when the whole market falls.
    • {% if etf_kind == 'Crypto' %}
  • Crypto prices are highly volatile and can move sharply in a single day.
  • You own fund shares, not coins. You can't move them to a crypto wallet.
  • Crypto trades around the clock, but these funds trade only during US market hours, so prices can jump when the market opens.
  • Spot bitcoin and ether funds aren't registered under the Investment Company Act of 1940 like most ETFs, so they come with fewer of that law's investor protections.
    • {% else %}
  • Funds focused on one area, such as small companies or technology, usually swing more than broad-market funds.
  • Funds holding non-US companies are also affected by currency moves.
    • {% endif %}
  • Past performance doesn't predict future returns. This guide is general information, not personal financial advice.
